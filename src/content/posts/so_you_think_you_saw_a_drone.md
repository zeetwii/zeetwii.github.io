## So you think you saw a drone?

A couple weeks ago, we started getting more news articles claiming drone sightings within CONUS (Continental United States) [military bases](https://abcnews.com/International/multiple-waves-unauthorized-drones-spotted-strategic-us-air/story?id=131245527).  This has spawned a lot of panic and speculation about what these drones could be, who is flying them, and what their intentions are.  However, most of these sightings are likely misidentified objects rather than actual drones.  I've done incident response to drone sightings for years, and I can tell you that the vast majority of these reports turn out to be false alarms.  In this post, I'll explain why most drone sightings are misidentifications and what you can do to avoid falling for the same trap.

### It has lights, how hard can this be?

People tend to assume that if they see something with lights in the sky, it must be a drone.  However, especially in US airspace, there are many objects that can have lights and be mistaken for drones.  For example, airplanes, helicopters, weather balloons, satellites, and even stars all either have lights or illuminate and have all been mistaken for drones at some point.  People don't normally look up at the sky, and so when they do, they will often misinterpret what they see.  Figuring out how far away and how large an object is can be surprisingly difficult, especially at night.  A small drone flying close to you can look very similar to a large airplane flying far away.  This even extends to movements, people will often mistake a aircraft for drones because they misinterpret the movements of the object and assume it is hovering or moving too slowly to be anything else.  

There is a classic tell for these kinds of misidentifications, which is that the meme of standing there menacingly.  In the case reported by ABC News, the witnesses are claiming that a swarm of drones have repeatedly violated US military airspace for the sole purpose of hovering with their lights on while base security panics.  This is not how actual drone attacks work, and not something any threat actor would do.  Drones are generally used for either surveillance or attacks, and in either case, they would not be hovering in place with their lights on for extended periods of time.  No malicious actor would want to draw attention to themselves by hovering in place with their lights on, especially in a high-security area like a military base.  

This behavior does match the idea of someone misidentify a plane or helicopter, and because of how far away the aircraft actually is, it appears to the observer to be hovering or doing some form of holding pattern.  It's actually pretty difficult for most people to guess the distance and speed of an airborne object, especially at night or with low viability.  Back when I would do C-UAS missions, we would regularly conflict reports by reviewing local ADS-B traffic in the area to ensure that there was not a risk of misidentification before deploying field equipment.  

### It's a bird, it's a plane, it's a drone, no one knows

The one valid complaint from a lot of these reports is that there is not a good or easy way for the average American to look up and know what's above them at any given moment.  Yes, there are apps that track stars, and apps that track planes, and the FAA is working on some way to show or alert for drones using remote ID, but all of them are separate.  The average person doesn't really look up that often, and there's enough going on in the sky now that it's not completely fair to dunk on the individual for panicking or being confused.  A good example of this is a couple of years ago during the big UFO scare in the East Coast, the former governor of Maryland made a video post complaining about unknown objects in the sky and pointed to what he thought was one of them.  Instead, this was a star, and he became a bit of a joke on the internet because of that.  His screwup is a great example of why we should consider having a easier to search combined database of known objects, and possible even an app managed by the federal government to simplify access to it.  

Right now regulating and identifying who or what is above you falls to one of three groups: NASA, FAA, and the DoD.  If the object is not in orbit and commercial, i.e. a plane, helicopter, or drone, the FAA has authority over it, with a small exception for military systems, which would be covered by both the FAA and DoD depending on when and if they are in military airspace or not.  Stuff in orbit is managed by NASA, and to their credit, NASA has done a great job at making tracking and viewing orbital objects pretty accessible.  Unfortunately, the rest of the federal government has dropped the ball, and the sharing of tracks or allowing real time lookups is an infrastructure category that has been left to rot on the vine.  Fear articles like the ABC piece are a great example of why we need to actually throw the money and resources at allowing better accessibility for both state, local, and civilians across these different databases, and we should consider trying to unify them into an easy to view end point for the average concerned citizen.
